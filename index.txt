Till Holzapfel

Mesmer Prism

 I study how people perceive, inhabit, and shape shared worlds.
 I am now a PhD candidate in mixed-ability human–swarm interaction.
 My approach, Combinatorial Swarmability, asks how people with different
 abilities can act through a shared, changing system and negotiate
 how their contributions matter. This grows out of my work with
 embodied perception, biofeedback, and luminous, porous virtual bodies.

 Based in the Inclusive Technology and Collective Engagement group (https://rc-trust.ai/groups/inclusive-technology-and-collective-engagement),

How can different abilities become shared possibilities?

 A shared interface does not have to give everyone the same controls.
 One person might shape a swarm's direction, another its rhythm or density,
 and another the rules by which it responds. The research question is how
 those contributions can become understandable, negotiable, and meaningful
 to the people making them.

 Combinatorial Swarmability (https://mesmerprism.com/projects/combinatorial-swarmability.html)
 is the focus of my PhD in mixed-ability human–swarm interaction, in
 Prof. Giulia Barbareschi's group. I plan to develop it through participatory
 design, shared virtual environments, and studies of how people coordinate
 and repair interaction. Swarms are a medium to explore; more equitable
 collaboration is a question to investigate, not an automatic result.

 One direction is a dynamic reflection of a group's meeting: a system whose
 behaviour helps participants discuss what is happening between them.
 Its meanings would have to be defined and revised with the group.
 This is an early research proposal, not a tool that can already measure
 inclusion or infer how people feel.

 Read the research direction and its foundations (https://mesmerprism.com/projects/combinatorial-swarmability.html)

From perception to relations between people

 My earlier work asks how a person interfaces with the world: how perception,
 expectation, and belief shape experience, and how an interface can change
 the felt boundary of the body. The new work carries those questions into
 the space between people.

 Viscereality (https://mesmerprism.com/projects/viscereality.html) brings breath and
 physiological feedback into changing visual systems. It is a practical
 meeting point for my interests in dynamical systems and neuroaesthetics:
 how the behaviour of a visual environment relates to bodily experience.

 Plasmatic Multitudes (https://mesmerprism.com/projects/plasmatic-multitudes.html)
 develops porous, particle-based bodies. My avatar work at the Intangible
 Realities Lab grew within its Isness work and collaboration with aNUma,
 in the wider context of numadelic aesthetics and shared embodiment.

 Rusty Morphospace (https://mesmerprism.com/projects/rusty-morphospace.html) is the
 open-source infrastructure thread: making immersive systems easier to
 build, operate, and inspect. Extending this work toward robotic systems,
 including Reachy Mini, is a planned next step.

Background

 I am a cognitive scientist, researcher, and developer working across HCI,
 psychophysics, immersive media, and artistic research. Much of my work has
 concerned the relationship between perception and interpretation: how
 attention, expectation, imagery, and belief affect the world we experience.
 The essays on mental imagery (https://mesmerprism.com/projects/prophantasia.html),
 phenomenological control (https://mesmerprism.com/projects/phenomenological-control.html),
 and scientific surrealism (https://mesmerprism.com/projects/scientific-surrealism.html)
 develop that strand.

 Building interactive systems gives these questions a material form.
 My work has included avatar development and public engagement at the
 Intangible Realities Lab (https://www.intangiblerealitieslab.org/),
 bioresponsive VR through Viscereality, and citizen-science infrastructure
 through the Altered Xperience Project. Open tools make the apparatus easier
 to inspect and adapt, while research writing makes its assumptions and
 limits available for discussion.

 My PhD shifts the emphasis toward collective interaction: who can act,
 how actions combine, and how people negotiate the rules of a shared
 environment. The earlier questions about perception and embodiment remain,
 now joined by questions about access, interdependence, and shared authorship.
